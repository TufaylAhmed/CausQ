NIS2, DORA and the year compliance got real.

Regulation just moved from “best efforts” to personal, board-level accountability. Here’s what actually changed.

For years, security regulation was something legal teams managed and engineers mostly ignored. With NIS2 and DORA, that era is over. Two things changed at once: the scope widened dramatically, and accountability moved to the top of the house.

The two shifts that matter

  • Scope. NIS2 pulls in far more sectors and their supply chains. Organisations that never considered themselves “critical infrastructure” now fall squarely in scope, and so do their vendors.
  • Accountability. Management bodies are now explicitly responsible for cyber-risk, with real consequences for failing to govern it. “We outsourced that” is no longer a defence.

DORA adds a financial-sector lens: operational resilience, rigorous third-party risk management, and the ability to detect, respond to and report incidents within tight windows. Both regimes share a theme , you must be able to prove your posture, not merely assert it.

The regulators stopped asking whether you have a policy. They started asking you to demonstrate it works, under a clock.

What “ready” looks like in practice

Most of what these regulations demand is good engineering you wanted anyway: an accurate asset and data inventory, network segmentation that limits blast radius, identity-first access, real telemetry, tested incident response, and honest visibility into your supply chain. The compliance artefact is a by-product of running the network well, not a separate binder.

A useful self-test: if a regulator asked you to evidence how an incident would be detected, contained and reported within the mandated window, today, with your current tooling , could you show them, not tell them?

The opportunity

Treated as a checkbox, NIS2 and DORA are a cost. Treated as a forcing function, they’re the budget and board attention you needed to fix posture debt that’s been deferred for years. The smart move is to map the regulatory requirements onto a modernisation programme you’d want regardless, and let compliance fund the upgrade.

This article is general commentary, not legal advice. Obligations vary by member state, sector and entity classification, confirm specifics with qualified counsel.
